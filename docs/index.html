What actually gets installed
Cover
Xray serving VLESS + XHTTP + REALITY on 443/tcp, behind a real site on your own domain with a real certificate. A prober that knocks gets the site — because it is the site. It protects the two-server link and phones using a VLESS app. On one server, WireGuard devices connect as plain WireGuard.
Devices
Plain WireGuard, official apps. Nothing custom to install on a phone or a laptop.
Panel
A small web panel on the server: add a device, get a QR, see bytes and last-seen. The panel and push alerts are in Russian in this version; everything Claude says to you, and the handout you pass on, are in your language.
Watchdog
A service that checks the tunnel every 30 seconds, restarts what it can and alerts your phone. On two servers it also moves everyone to a spare route.
Split routing
Two-server layout only: an nftables set decides who goes through the tunnel, and a domain list you edit leaves directly from the relay. Seeded with the payment networks that usually object — add your own bank.
Layouts
One server abroad by default, or a two-machine relay when devices sit behind heavy filtering. Same installers either way.

Why your own server

Every VPN you've been sold is someone else's server. This one is yours.

Commercial VPNs share an IP among thousands of strangers, so you inherit their reputation: banks, shops and streaming services flag the address as "VPN" and you get captchas, refused logins and toggling the app on and off. That's the shape of the business. Your own server gets rid of the strangers, but it still sits in a data centre, and some services flag that too.

A server of your own is one IP, used by the people you know. Nobody in the middle sees your traffic, nobody can sell it, and nobody can change the terms. And it costs what a server costs — about $6 a month — not $6 a month per person.

One honest limit: some carrier and corporate networks restrict VPN traffic altogether, and no setup on your side changes that. Burrow tells you when the network, not the setup, is the problem.

Your own addressUsed by the people you added, not by thousands of strangers. On the default one-server layout everything leaves from it, and it is a data-centre address: some banks still challenge those. On the two-server layout, apps that refuse VPN connections leave from the home-country relay and keep working.
PrivateYour own server, so nobody sees your traffic. Access logs and DNS logging are off. What is kept is bytes and last-seen per device, plus a 14-day history so the panel can draw a graph — all of it on your server. You will see that a phone used 2 GB; nobody sees what it opened, because that is never recorded. Privacy, stated plainly.
FastNo shared bandwidth and no queue of strangers on the same address: the server's whole pipe belongs to the handful of devices you added.
Whose machineNothing of yours runs on my machines. The server is rented in your name, billed to your card, at a host you pick — and Burrow runs no servers at all, so there is no account to close.

Paying for the server

Card declined?

It happens — some hosts reject prepaid or debit cards, or a card from a bank they don't know. You can still rent a server. Three ways round it, in the order I'd try them.

01 Someone else pays for you

A friend or relative whose card the provider accepts rents the server. About $6 a month. They can keep paying it, or you pay them back.

02 Hosts that take crypto or local payment methods

The guide keeps a dated list of hosts that take PayPal, crypto or local methods. None are tested with Burrow yet, so check before you pay.

See the list in the guide

03 Virtual-card services

They work — and they're last on this list for a reason.

Fees, identity checks, and they come and go. I don't partner with any of them or take a cut — check one yourself before trusting it with a card.
