Why your own server

Every VPN you've been sold has one thing to block. Yours has none.

Commercial VPNs share an IP among thousands of strangers. When one address gets flagged, all of them go dark, and the app-store listing goes with it. That's not a bug in a particular app — it's the shape of the business.

A server of your own is one IP, used by the people you know, behind a domain nobody has a reason to look at. There's no company to pressure, no listing to remove, no subscription to cancel. And it costs what a server costs — about $6 a month — not $6 a month per person.

Honest limit: a network with a strict allow-list can block everything foreign, and no protocol fixes that. Burrow tells you when it can't help.

Shared vs yoursShared servers get blocked because thousands of strangers share the address. A personal one has nothing to flag.
Whose machineNothing runs on anyone else's infrastructure. The server is rented in your name and stays yours — Burrow has no servers to run.
Who sees trafficNobody sees your browsing. Access logs are off, and the only numbers that exist are bytes and last-seen per device.
Split tunnelingYour bank works without toggling anything. Local sites go direct, the rest through the tunnel.

Do you have a card that works outside Russia?

If not, the server is still yours to rent — there are three ways round it, in the order I'd try them.

01 Someone abroad pays

A friend or relative with a working card rents the machine. It's about $6 a month, and the account can be handed to you afterwards.

02 Hosts that take crypto or regional cards

Plenty of providers accept payment methods that work where you are. The guide lists which ones are known to work and what to watch for.

See the list in the guide

03 Foreign-card services

Services that issue a card you can pay with. They work, and they are the last option for a reason.

Fees, identity checks, and they come and go. We don't partner with any of them and don't take a cut — check one yourself before you trust it with a card.
